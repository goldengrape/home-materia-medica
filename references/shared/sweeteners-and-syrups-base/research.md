# Research Parent — Syrups, Sugar Ingredients, and Sweeteners

Evidence cutoff: 2026-09-24

## Scope and identity

This parent covers syrup ingredients, instant-noodle seasoning sachets, sugar alcohols and high-intensity sweeteners. These are separate food forms and chemical classes. A sweetener ingredient does not define the full nutrition profile of the food that contains it. Check the ingredient list, nutrition label, portion size, and market-specific rules.

Do not treat:
- corn syrup as a synonym for high-fructose corn syrup;
- Chinese fructose-glucose syrup as identical to every US HFCS grade without checking the applicable standard and product label;
- maple syrup as identical to maple-flavoured table syrup;
- sugar alcohols as high-intensity sweeteners or as interchangeable with each other;
- “sugar-free,” “reduced sugar,” or “sweetened with a substitute” as proof of low energy, low carbohydrate, diabetes suitability, weight loss, or disease prevention;
- sodium in an instant-noodle package as a measured sodium value for its seasoning sachet alone.

## Product and label evidence

A China study of instant-noodle consumers combined special sodium monitoring with 2017–2020 consumption survey data. Its modeled scenario using one-third of the seasoning packet lowered total dietary sodium for the modeled consumer group by 6.74% compared with the baseline scenario. This is a population-model scenario and does not identify a universal sachet sodium value. A separate ten-country label survey analyzed 765 noodle products and found broad between-country variation; package totals should not be assigned to the seasoning packet alone.

China’s GB/T 20882.4-2021 is a current recommended national standard for high-fructose syrup (果葡糖浆). In US federal regulation, corn syrup is produced by partial hydrolysis of corn starch and may contain glucose, maltose, and higher saccharides; common US HFCS grades are defined separately. Maple syrup should be distinguished from flavoured table syrups.

## Sweetener classes and tolerance

Erythritol and xylitol are polyols (sugar alcohols). A systematic review found dose-dependent malabsorption and gastrointestinal symptoms across polyols, with individual compounds, dose, combinations, and participant groups varying. This category-level evidence does not establish one universal tolerance threshold. High-intensity sweeteners such as steviol glycosides and sucralose are chemically and functionally distinct; regulator conditions apply to specified ingredients and uses. JECFA’s steviol-glycoside ADI is expressed as steviol, so it cannot be compared directly with the mass of every commercial extract.

China’s 2025 new-food-ingredient announcement permits D-allulose subject to specified conditions: recommended use not above 20 g/day, with infants, pregnant people, and breastfeeding people listed as unsuitable, and required label statements. A 2026 small 30-day study of 50 healthy adults tested 24 or 36 g/day. It reported mild, transient gastrointestinal symptoms in 48%; some blood measures changed and the high-dose group aged 35 years or older had lower bone-mineral-density T-scores, all within clinical reference ranges. These short-term results do not supersede China’s 20 g/day condition or establish long-term safety. FDA’s allulose guidance is a US nutrition-label policy and must not be substituted for China’s rules.

WHO’s free-sugars guidance concerns total dietary exposure. Its 2023 non-sugar-sweetener guideline is a conditional public-health recommendation about long-term weight-control and noncommunicable-disease risk strategies; it is not an assessment that an individual approved sweetener or food is toxic.

## Limits and open questions

Studies of labels, ingredient identity, regulatory review, short-term tolerance, and long-term clinical outcomes answer different questions. Current brand recipes and actual serving exposure change over time. Do not infer a product-level benefit or risk from ingredient class alone.
