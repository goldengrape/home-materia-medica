# Research Parent — Sweetened, Sugar-Reduced, and Sweetener-Substituted Desserts

Evidence cutoff: 2026-09-24

## Scope and identity

This parent covers packaged or prepared desserts marketed as low-sugar, sugar-free, reduced-sugar, or sweetened with substitutes. These labels describe different things. A product may reduce sucrose but retain other sugars; use a non-sugar sweetener; substitute a polyol; use a fermentable fibre such as short-chain fructo-oligosaccharides (scFOS); or simply meet a nutrition-claim threshold. The ingredients, energy, available carbohydrate, sweetness, serving size, and food matrix therefore need to be checked product by product.

Do not treat:
- “low sugar” as synonymous with “low energy,” “low carbohydrate,” “diabetes-safe,” or “beneficial for weight loss”;
- high-intensity non-sugar sweeteners as interchangeable with polyols or fibres;
- an acute response to one reformulated dessert as evidence for all sugar-substituted desserts;
- a low-glycemic-index (GI) dessert as a generic low-sugar dessert;
- results for people with diagnosed diabetes as automatically generalizable to the general population.

## Human evidence

Lecerf et al. studied two specific products in healthy adults: dairy desserts and pound cakes in which scFOS replaced 30% of the sugar. In separate double-blind crossover tests, the dairy dessert produced lower 0–120 min postprandial glucose and insulin area-under-the-curve values; the pound cake did not significantly alter glucose AUC and showed only a nonsignificant tendency toward lower insulin AUC. The authors noted that food-matrix features such as fat may modify the result. These findings concern the tested recipes and acute surrogate outcomes, not a general effect of “代糖甜点.”

Argiana et al. studied 51 people with type 2 diabetes after an overnight fast. Participants consumed either 83 g of a conventional dessert or 150 g of a low-GI/low-glycemic-load dessert in random order. Postprandial glucose and glucose incremental AUC were lower after the low-GI/GL dessert; insulin incremental AUC and triglycerides did not differ. Hunger/fullness ratings favored the low-GI/GL dessert. The portions differed, the observation was acute, and the trial did not test long-term glycemic control or weight loss. It is direct evidence for the two tested desserts in that population, not proof that all desserts bearing a “low sugar” claim have the same response.

## Guidance on non-sugar sweeteners

The WHO guideline advises against using non-sugar sweeteners as a long-term strategy for weight control or prevention of diet-related noncommunicable disease in the general population. The recommendation is conditional and is not based on toxicological assessments of individual sweeteners; it does not replace JECFA or other authoritative acceptable-intake guidance. The guideline does not apply to people with pre-existing diabetes and does not establish that a particular dessert is unsafe.

## Chinese packaged-food nutrition claims

At this evidence cutoff, GB 28050—2011 remains applicable until the transition to GB 28050—2025. The official 2011 standard, Appendix C, Table C.1, permits the “低糖” claim at no more than 5 g sugars per 100 g for solids or per 100 mL for liquids. Claims must be assessed on the packaged product and its nutrition declaration, not inferred from words such as “light,” “zero,” or “sugar substitute.”

The National Health Commission announced GB 28050—2025 in 2025. Its official Q&A states that the revised nutrition-labelling standard takes effect on 2027-03-16 and that nutrition claims must meet the requirements and wording in Appendix C. For products made on or after that effective date, check the new standard and current label; do not assume every 2011 claim threshold continues unchanged.

## Limits and open questions

Evidence for commercial “sugar substitute desserts” as a broad category is sparse and formulation-specific. Longer-term effects, serving-size compensation, palatability, gastrointestinal tolerance of polyols/fibres, and outcomes across people with different glycemic status remain product- and dose-dependent. Treat label compliance, acute glycemic response, and long-term health effect as separate questions.
