## Product identity and processing

Cocoa-derived foods are not one chemically stable exposure. Cocoa beans undergo fermentation and drying before later industrial steps such as shell removal, roasting, grinding, pressing, and sometimes alkalization (“Dutching”). Cocoa powder is the defatted or partly defatted solid fraction after cocoa butter is pressed from cocoa liquor; drinking cocoa mixes may additionally contain sugar, milk powder, or other ingredients. Chocolate products recombine cocoa solids and cocoa butter with different amounts of sugar, dairy, nuts, and other fats. White chocolate has a distinct regulatory composition in the United States: cocoa butter and milk/sweeteners, with no nonfat cacao solids. A sweet sauce or nut spread is a composite condiment, not equivalent to cocoa powder or a chocolate bar.

Fermentation, drying, roasting, pressing, and alkalization change aroma, moisture, fat, and flavanol composition. In a commercial powder analysis, average total flavanols were reported as 34.6 mg/g for natural cocoa, 13.8 for lightly alkalized, 7.8 for medium alkalized, and 3.9 for heavily alkalized powders. This is analytical composition evidence, not a clinical health outcome; the paper reports author affiliations with a cocoa manufacturer and an analytical laboratory, which should remain visible as context.

## Human evidence transfer

Human trials often use a standardized cocoa drink, an experimentally enriched chocolate, isolated flavanols, or an acute biomarker. Those exposures cannot be transferred automatically to ordinary retail cocoa powder, milk chocolate, white chocolate, syrup, nama chocolate, or cocoa-hazelnut spread. The relevant variables include exact food matrix, dose, processing, comparator, serving, duration, participant group, and endpoint. A short-term vascular or pharmacokinetic biomarker is not a disease outcome. A control chocolate in a trial does not by itself establish a benefit for the control food.

No traditional entry has been identified in this parent that directly classifies current branded cocoa products. Traditional descriptions of other foods, isolated ingredients, or later culinary uses must not be rewritten as historical claims about these products.

## Food safety and storage

Cocoa beans are agricultural products, and safe handling depends on postharvest fermentation, drying, storage, and manufacturing controls. The Codex cocoa code describes contamination and ochratoxin A prevention practices. This establishes that production control matters; it does not mean a particular finished chocolate is contaminated. For products with cream and high moisture, the name “fresh” should be read as a product/texture identity, not proof of reduced processing or special health value. The label’s storage and use-by instructions govern the specific product.

Common practical safety variables are added sugar, saturated fat, dairy or nut allergens, product-specific storage, and serving size. A sauce, sweet spread, and chocolate bar may share cocoa ingredients while delivering very different amounts of sugar, fat, and cocoa solids.
