# Research Parent — Protein Foods, Meal Replacements, and Fiber Ingredients

Evidence cutoff: 2026-09-24

## Scope and identity

Protein powders and shakes, full or partial meal replacements, high-protein desserts, and fiber ingredients are different product classes. A health outcome from a protein supplement during supervised resistance training does not describe a high-protein pudding or every drink. A meal replacement is defined by intended use and product formulation, not by a bar or shake shape. Fiber types differ in viscosity, fermentation and evidence.

Check the full ingredient list and nutrition declaration, serving size, protein or fiber source, energy, total sugars, saturated fat, sodium, allergens, and intended use. In China, pre-packaged foods made before the transition date remain subject to the nutrition-labelling rules then in force; GB 28050-2025 is scheduled to take effect on 2027-03-16. A compliant “high protein” claim indicates a content threshold, not a clinical result.

## Protein supplement evidence

A network meta-analysis of 116 randomized trials involving 4,711 adults undergoing resistance training included casein, whey, soy, mixed proteins, and other sources. Protein source and timing were evaluated within a training program. Some lean-mass and strength outcomes improved, while the measured physical-function tests did not. A 2018 meta-analysis of protein supplementation with resistance training found modest additional gains, with a plateau estimate near 1.6 g/kg/day of total protein intake; the amount is a study estimate, not a universal prescription.

A 2026 review of 18 randomized trials (1,893 adults) comparing plant and animal protein supplements for at least six months reported no statistically significant differences across several outcomes; included plant proteins were largely soy. The review includes authors affiliated with Danone Research and Innovation, and the studies were heterogeneous. These findings should be read alongside shorter resistance-training trials and not treated as proof that every protein isolate or whole-food source is interchangeable.

Meal replacement evidence concerns structured weight-management programs. A 2019 systematic review included 23 randomized trials and 8,253 adults. In a subset with comparable behavioral support, meal-replacement programs produced greater average weight loss at one year than comparator diet programs, but estimates were heterogeneous. Trials included shakes, bars, portion-controlled meals, and other foods; this does not establish an independent effect for a particular bar or drink.

## Dietary fiber evidence

A 2022 meta-analysis of 16 randomized trials and 1,251 adults with chronic constipation reported improved response and stool frequency with fiber supplementation, especially in trials using more than 10 g/day and at least four weeks. The clearest type-specific evidence was for psyllium and pectin, while flatulence increased. Do not apply those results to every fiber ingredient.

Inulin-type fructans are fermentable fibers. A 2025 randomized trial of 39 adults with functional constipation tested 12 g/day of chicory inulin for four weeks. A separate review in adults with irritable bowel and functional bowel disorders found that inulin-type fructans can worsen flatulence; dose and baseline gut sensitivity matter. A food with added inulin is not thereby a constipation treatment.

A 2026 randomized trial tested a manufacturer’s resistant dextrin product at 15 g/day for four weeks in 124 healthy, normal-weight men. Fecal Parabacteroides increased during use, then returned toward baseline after washout. Stool frequency, consistency, and measured short-chain fatty acids did not significantly change. The manufacturer funded and participated in study design, oversight and manuscript preparation; some authors were manufacturer employees. This short-term microbiome result is not a clinical health outcome.

## Limits and open questions

Product formulations, food sources, protein and fiber doses, total diet, exercise, disease status and duration all alter exposure. Neither marketing claims nor evidence about an isolated ingredient can determine whether a specific commercial product is appropriate for an individual.
